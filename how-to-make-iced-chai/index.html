How to make iced chai that isn't watery

Almost every weak iced chai has the same cause, and it isn't the recipe. It's what happens between the pot and the glass.

The problem

Hot chai poured straight over ice melts roughly a third of that ice on contact. You've just added a third of a glass of water to a drink you spent eight minutes concentrating. Then it sits, and the rest of the ice keeps going.

Cold also mutes flavour. The same cup that tasted balanced hot will taste thin and under-spiced at fridge temperature — your tongue simply picks up less. So iced chai has to start stronger than you think, twice over.

Chai ice cubes are the whole trick

Freeze a batch of chai in an ice tray and use those instead of water ice. As they melt they add chai, not water, so the last mouthful is as strong as the first. It costs you nothing but a tray and some forethought, and it is the single biggest improvement you can make.

Brew a double batch, drink half hot, freeze the rest. A tray keeps for about a month before it starts picking up freezer smells.

Don't pour it hot

Chill the chai properly before it meets ice. In the fridge that's two or three hours; if you're in a hurry, stand the pot in a sink of cold water and stir, which takes about fifteen minutes. Pouring hot over ice is the mistake that causes the problem in the first place.

Never chill it uncovered next to anything strong-smelling. Milk takes on odours fast.

Sweeten while it's hot

Sugar barely dissolves in cold liquid — it sinks and sits at the bottom, so the first sip is bland and the last is syrup. Sweeten the pot while it's still hot, taste it warm, and remember it will read slightly less sweet once cold. If you prefer to sweeten later, use a simple syrup rather than granulated.

Which milk

Whole milk gives the best body cold, where thin milk shows up immediately. Oat is the best of the plant milks here — it stays creamy chilled and doesn't split when it hits acidic spice. Almond tends to separate and go chalky. If you're using oat, the barista versions are worth it for exactly this reason.

Getting the strength right

Aim for a hot cup that tastes slightly too strong — a bit tannic, a bit too spiced. That's the right starting point. Cold and dilution will pull it back to balanced. If it tastes perfect hot, it will taste weak cold, every time.

In practice that means about half the water and the same everything else. Our hot brewing guide has the base method; this is that, concentrated.

Common mistakes

  • Pouring hot chai over ice. The cause of most watery iced chai.
  • Normal-strength brew. Right hot means wrong cold.
  • Sweetening cold. The sugar never dissolves.
  • Water ice. Every minute it sits, it gets weaker.
  • Leaving the spices in while it chills. Cloves and cassia keep extracting in the fridge and will take over by morning. Strain first.
